## Working Title
**From "Problem Student" to Contextual Learner: Deconstructing Behavioral Stereotypes Through Classroom Observation**

---

## Introduction

This paper examines a bias I have carried into classroom practice: the stereotype that some students are inherently "difficult," "off-task," or "behavior problems." For this assignment, I focus on students in my elementary practicum placement and analyze in-person classroom interactions documented through direct observation. I chose this setting because it provides concrete, lived observational material from my actual work as an educator-in-training, and because the moments I am analyzing were moments I participated in — not moments I watched from the outside.

The specific bias under investigation is the tendency to read visible frustration, noise, avoidance, or emotional escalation as evidence of fixed student deficiency. My working question is: **How does observing students who might otherwise be labeled as difficult or off-task challenge the stereotype that behavior problems are fixed traits rather than responses to confusion, frustration, and insufficient support?**

My central claim is that what looks like "bad behavior" is often better understood as a contextual response to unclear expectations, cognitive overload, time pressure, or insufficient instructional scaffolding. When the instructional conditions change, student behavior changes too — and that shift is precisely what deconstructs the myth of the fixed "problem student."

## Section 1 — Autobiographical Grounding / Naming the Bias

This paper is not written from a neutral position. I am writing as a student teacher who is simultaneously learning classroom management and learning to question the interpretive habits I bring into that work. The bias I am examining is not only something I have observed in others; it is a habit of interpretation that I recognize in my own instincts and that I need to examine honestly.

The stereotype I am interrogating is the idea that certain students are naturally disruptive, lazy, difficult, or unmotivated — and that these labels reflect something true and stable about who those students are. In school spaces, those labels can appear very quickly. When a child becomes loud, frustrated, resistant, or visibly overwhelmed, those behaviors are public and immediate. Because they are so visible, they can feel like evidence of character rather than evidence of struggle.

I grew up in a context where compliance was treated as intelligence and where students who moved efficiently through instructions were read as "good." Students who needed more time, more explanation, or more emotional support were quietly sorted into a different category — not always explicitly, but through the texture of how teachers responded to them. I absorbed those frameworks without fully realizing it. Part of why I am drawn to examining behavioral stereotypes specifically is that I have already caught myself, more than once, having a first instinctive reaction to a student's behavior before I have asked what is underneath it.

This is the kind of socially learned bias that is easy to carry invisibly into the classroom — not out of malice, but out of the way schooling conditions us to read behavior. Naming it is the first step toward not reproducing it.

## Section 3 — Myth vs. Reality (Core Analysis)

### The Myth

Some students are "problem students" because they are naturally disruptive, unmotivated, or unwilling to follow directions. This belief treats behavior as a window into a student's character or capacity rather than as a response to conditions.

### The Reality

My classroom observations challenge that framing directly. In the grade 4 coding observation, student frustration appeared almost immediately when students were tasked with a multi-step coding challenge they had not yet fully processed. Students expressed confusion verbally, physically became restless, and asked questions in a way that could easily be read as disruptive. But what the transcript of that session makes clear is that many students had not yet understood what they were trying to do. The directions had been delivered, but the cognitive scaffolding had not yet arrived.

The shift that followed is the most analytically important part of the observation. When the task was broken down step by step, the route was counted aloud, students were actively reminded not to rush, and the teacher presence became more relational and explicit — the same students who had appeared frustrated and off-task began to re-engage. They returned to the problem. They started counting. They started troubleshooting.

The behavior had not changed because the students changed. The behavior changed because the instructional conditions changed. This is the deconstructive moment: the myth locates the problem inside the student; the observation locates the problem in the interaction between task design, pacing, clarity of expectations, and available support.

The college-level seminar discussion reinforces this structural point from a different angle. In that session, a facilitator reflected directly on the way class inequality shapes what gets counted as student effort:

> "There's a lot of things that we are also impacting decisions... It may not be that one kid is just better than another — but what we're asking them to do is second class in education."

The facilitator gave the example of a poster project at a high-socioeconomic-status school where one student brought in a professionally supported, elaborate project — not because she was more talented, but because someone in her household had the time, transportation, and resources to help her execute it. Another student, just as capable, brought in a simpler version because they had to make it alone. The teacher's first instinct, reading only the product, was to infer something about the students themselves. The seminar pushed back on that inference: what looked like a difference in students was actually a difference in support structures.

This connects directly to the behavioral stereotype. When a student cannot execute what is asked of them — whether because of unclear directions, missing scaffolding, absent resources, or cognitive overload — the visible output can look like disengagement, laziness, or defiance. The stereotype reads the behavior and labels the student. A structural lens reads the same behavior and asks what conditions produced it.

## Section 4 — Assets and Funds of Knowledge

An asset-based reading of the same observations reveals what the behavioral stereotype would otherwise erase. The students in the coding challenge observation are not empty, passive, or incapable. They are reasoning, testing, counting aloud, speaking through possibilities, and trying again. Even when frustrated, they remain in the problem space. The frustration itself is evidence of investment — you do not get frustrated by something you are indifferent to.

In the seminar discussion, one of the instructors captured this well when talking about inquiry-based learning:

> "Treat your kids like they are agents. Give them some agency to go find out — what is the big problem? How can you fix it? Be shocked at what they come up with."

This reframing is not naive optimism. It is a deliberate pedagogical choice to start from the student's existing capacity rather than from their visible deficit. Students who "act out" in coding tasks often know exactly what they want to do — they are frustrated because the tool, the time, or the explanation is not yet matching what they need.

The funds of knowledge framework suggests that every student walks into the classroom with rich experiential resources — knowledge from home, community, lived experience — that formal schooling often fails to activate or recognize. In a CS/STEM context, those funds can include spatial reasoning, troubleshooting logic, storytelling, collaboration habits, and cultural knowledge about how things are built and repaired. When we read a student as a "behavior problem" rather than as a person with a set of funds that have not yet been activated by the task, we are making a knowledge-erasure move dressed up as classroom management.

## Section 5 — Implications for Future Practice

This observation changes what I want to do before I assign a label to behavior. My practical commitments going forward are:

- **Pause before I categorize.** When I notice a student who is escalating, frustrated, or disengaged, my first question is: what is the instructional condition that may be producing this? Is the student confused? Are the expectations clear? Is the pacing too fast? Is the task asking for more than the current scaffold allows?

- **Scaffold before I correct.** The coding observation showed clearly that the same students who appeared disruptive became productive learners once the task was broken into smaller, visible steps. That is a pedagogical move, not a discipline move. It should come first.

- **Watch for class and resource asymmetry.** Following the seminar discussion on systemic inequity, I want to audit the assignments I give for built-in advantages — tasks that assume students have adult help at home, access to supplies, time, or prior vocabulary exposure that some students simply do not have. Treating unequal outputs as evidence of unequal students is how the stereotype reproduces itself.

- **Make behavior a data point, not a conclusion.** Student behavior tells me something. It does not tell me everything. The story I construct around a behavior is my interpretation, and interpretations can be revised when I gather more information about conditions.

Sonia Nieto's framework of affirming diversity calls on teachers to actively question deficit thinking — the habit of explaining underperformance through student characteristics (laziness, apathy, disorganization) rather than through the structural conditions that shape student experience. The behavioral stereotype is a form of deficit thinking because it locates the problem in the student's inherent nature rather than in the interaction between the student and the instructional environment.

Bell hooks, writing about engaged pedagogy, argues that real teaching requires the teacher to recognize students as whole subjects rather than as objects to be processed through a system. When I reflexively label behavior, I am treating the student as a type rather than as a person. Engaged pedagogy requires me to stay curious about the person underneath the behavior.

## Section 6 — Conclusion

I began this paper with a bias that I could easily have left unnamed: the habit of treating behavioral difficulty as a window into fixed student character. Classroom observation gave me the evidence I needed to deconstruct that habit.

The observation showed that the same students who appeared to be "problem students" became engaged learners when instructional conditions changed. That shift does not happen if the problem is located inside the student. It only makes sense if the problem is understood as relational — produced through the interaction between what the student brings and what the learning environment offers in return.

The seminar discussion on class and systemic inequity added the structural layer that observation alone cannot provide. Behavioral stereotypes do not operate in a vacuum. They are entangled with class-based assumptions about what support students are assumed to have, what vocabulary they are assumed to carry, what kinds of intelligence the task is designed to recognize. Deconstructing the behavioral stereotype means deconstructing those structural assumptions at the same time.

I cannot exit my own positionality. I carry histories, habits, and interpretive frameworks that will continue to shape my first reactions in the classroom. What I can do is build a practice of slowing down between my first reading of behavior and my response to it — and using the time in between to ask better questions. That ongoing discipline is, I think, what it means to actually apply this work.
