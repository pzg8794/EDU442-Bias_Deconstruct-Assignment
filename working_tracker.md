### 1.2 Bias focus
- [x] ~~Name the bias/prejudice/stereotype being examined~~

**Bias / prejudice / stereotype being examined**  
The stereotype that certain students are inherently “behavior problems,” “difficult,” or “unmotivated,” rather than students who may be confused, overwhelmed, under-supported, or responding to unclear expectations.

**Where this assumption may have come from**  
This assumption likely comes from school cultures that interpret visible frustration, noise, avoidance, or emotional dysregulation as signs of defiance or poor attitude. Students who struggle publicly often get read through a deficit lens, especially when they do not move smoothly through directions or independent work. Over time, those situational struggles can harden into informal labels about who a student “is,” instead of prompting questions about what support is missing.

**Why this matters for current or future work**  
This matters because if I accept the stereotype too quickly, I may misread student behavior as character failure rather than communication. My future work in education depends on learning to ask what is happening instructionally and relationally before I decide what is happening behaviorally.

**Who was present**  
Classroom teacher(s), students, and me in my teaching-placement role.

**What activity occurred**  
A Minecraft Education coding / agent challenge in which students had to read instructions, debug, count steps, and complete a timed dual-plate task.

**What the environment was like**  
The environment was active, noisy, and at points visibly frustrating for students. Several students appeared confused or overwhelmed by the task demands.

**Myths / assumptions I noticed myself carrying in**  
The strongest assumption was that students who become loud, frustrated, or off-task during complex work may be “behavior problems” rather than students reacting to confusion or inadequate scaffolding.

**What challenged those assumptions**  
The March 12 transcript shows that when the task is broken down, directions are clarified, pacing is slowed, and encouragement is given, student behavior shifts. In that transcript, frustration gives way to persistence and eventual success once the support changes.

**Assets / funds of knowledge observed**  
Students demonstrated problem-solving, persistence, willingness to try again, verbal reasoning, and the ability to follow multi-step logic when support was clear enough.

**Why this matters for future practice**  
This matters because it directly affects how I will interpret and respond to behavior in my own classroom. I want to learn to see dysregulation and confusion as signals that call for better instructional support, not automatic deficit judgments.
