# Deconstructing Bias — Working Draft

## Working title
**From “Problem Student” to Contextual Learner: Deconstructing Behavioral Stereotypes Through Classroom Observation**

---

## Introduction

This paper examines a bias I have carried into classroom practice: the stereotype that some students are inherently “difficult,” “off-task,” or “behavior problems.” For this assignment, I focus on students in my elementary practicum classroom and analyze in-person classroom interactions documented in my teaching-placement transcripts. I chose this setting because it gives me concrete observational material from my actual work as an educator-in-training rather than requiring me to invent or reconstruct an outside observation.

The specific bias under investigation is the tendency to read visible frustration, noise, avoidance, or emotional escalation as evidence of fixed student deficiency. My working question is: **How does observing students who might otherwise be labeled as difficult or off-task challenge the stereotype that behavior problems are fixed traits rather than responses to confusion, frustration, and insufficient support?**

My central claim is that what looks like “bad behavior” is often better understood as a contextual response to unclear expectations, cognitive overload, time pressure, or insufficient instructional support. The transcript evidence from my placement suggests that when the instructional conditions change, student behavior changes too.

## 1. Autobiographical grounding / naming the bias

This paper is not written from a neutral position. I am writing as a student teacher reflecting on how I interpret student behavior while learning to become a classroom teacher myself. The bias I am examining is not simply “other people’s prejudice”; it is also a habit of interpretation that I need to question in my own professional development.

The stereotype I am examining is the idea that certain students are naturally disruptive, lazy, difficult, or unmotivated. In school spaces, those labels often appear quickly whenever a child becomes loud, frustrated, resistant, or visibly overwhelmed. Because those behaviors are so public, they can seem like evidence of character rather than evidence of struggle. That is exactly what makes the stereotype so dangerous.

This assumption likely comes from the way schools often reward smooth compliance and treat visible difficulty as a problem to control. Students who move efficiently through instructions are read as “good,” while students who need more time, more explanation, or more emotional regulation support are often framed through a deficit lens. Over time, those momentary reactions become fixed stories about who a student is.

## 3. Myth vs. reality

### Myth
Some students are “problem students” because they are naturally disruptive, unmotivated, or unwilling to follow directions.

### Reality
The transcript complicates that stereotype. In the focal observation, student frustration is visible almost immediately. One student says, **“I’m gonna lose it,”** while the class as a whole is described as largely stuck in the task. The teacher explicitly notes that many students have not read the directions thoroughly and therefore do not fully understand what they are trying to do.

This matters because it shifts the interpretive frame. The problem is not simply that students are refusing to work; the problem is that the task is cognitively demanding, time-based, and confusing to many of them. The visible behavior appears before instructional clarity is fully established.

The most important pattern in the transcript is that behavior changes when support changes. Once the task is broken down step by step, students are reminded not to rush, the route is counted aloud, and the teacher support becomes more precise and relational, the same students begin to re-engage. The interaction becomes less about controlling behavior and more about making success reachable.

A particularly important instructional move in the transcript is the repeated slowing down of the task: counting steps aloud, resetting, visually orienting the student to the environment, and explicitly saying not to rush. By the end of one exchange, after the student gets through the challenge, the support closes with **“You did a good job.”** That moment matters because it reframes the student not as a behavioral problem, but as someone who was capable once the support matched the need.

This is the key deconstructive moment of the paper: the transcript suggests that the “problem” was not located in the student alone. It was produced through the interaction between task demands, timing pressure, directions, emotional regulation, and available support.

## 4. Assets and funds of knowledge

An asset-based reading of the same transcript reveals strengths that the stereotype would otherwise hide. The students in the observation are not empty, passive, or incapable. They are reasoning, testing, counting, troubleshooting, speaking through possibilities, and trying again. Even when frustrated, they remain engaged in the problem space.

What becomes visible through support is not only compliance, but competence. Students show persistence. They respond to concrete scaffolding. They can think logically through multi-step challenges when the path is made visible enough. They are also socially responsive: encouragement, calm pacing, and relational presence matter to how they work.

This section of the paper should resist turning the students into either victims or heroes. The point is not that they suddenly become exceptional once supported; the point is that the stereotype prevented their ordinary competence from being seen clearly in the first place.

## 5. Implications for future practice

This observation changes how I want to read behavior in my own classroom practice. I do not want to move too quickly from visible frustration to fixed labels. Instead, I want to ask a different set of questions:

- Is the student confused?
- Are the expectations clear enough?
- Is the pacing too fast?
- Is the task asking for more than the current support structure allows?
- What happens if I scaffold before I correct?

For my future work, this matters because behavior is one of the easiest places for bias to harden into routine judgment. If I accept the “problem student” stereotype too quickly, I risk participating in the very sorting practices I claim to oppose. If I slow down and examine context, I have a better chance of responding as an educator rather than merely reacting as a manager.

I also want to carry forward one specific lesson from the transcript: calm, structured, non-shaming support is not a soft alternative to rigor; it is one of the conditions that makes rigor possible. At the same time, I want to resist the impulse to interpret frustration as evidence of personal deficiency.
